Understanding the System

It's a formula.
Not a mystery.

FICO scores aren't arbitrary. They're calculated using five weighted factors that are entirely knowable — and two of them account for more than half your score.

Roughly 220 million Americans have credit scores. The average FICO score in 2024 was 717 — solidly in the "Good" range. But tens of millions sit below 650, paying dramatically higher rates for everything from car loans to apartment applications, losing thousands of dollars every year to a number they were never taught how to manage.

Here's what the credit industry doesn't advertise: the score is learnable. The inputs are fixed. The weights are public. And most people, if they take the right steps in the right order, can gain 50-100 points in 6 months or less — without paying a credit repair company a dime.

Part One Know the Levers
FICO Score Composition

The 5 factors that
determine your score

Before you can move the number, you need to know exactly what's moving it. Each factor has a different weight — and a different speed at which it responds to your actions.

35%
Payment History

The single largest factor. Whether you pay on time — every time — is the foundation of your score. One 30-day late payment can drop a 780 score by 90-110 points. The good news: it recovers, and the longer ago it was, the less it hurts. Recent on-time payments are the fastest way to demonstrate creditworthiness.

→ Set every account to autopay minimum. Never miss. Ever.
30%
Credit Utilization

How much of your available credit you're using. Keep it under 30%. For max score impact, aim for under 10%. This is the fastest-moving factor — a paydown can show results within one billing cycle.

→ Pay down balances before statement closing date, not just due date.
15%
Length of Credit History

Average age of all accounts, age of oldest account, and how recently new accounts were opened. This is the slow one. Time is the only cure, but you can protect it by keeping old accounts open, even unused.

→ Never close your oldest card. Use it once a year to keep it active.
10%
Credit Mix

Lenders like to see you can manage different types of credit: revolving (credit cards) and installment (auto, mortgage, personal loans). Having both is better than just one. Don't open accounts you don't need just for this.

→ A credit-builder loan from a credit union is an easy, low-risk add.
10%
New Credit / Hard Inquiries

Each time you apply for credit, a hard inquiry is recorded. It typically drops your score 5-10 points temporarily. Multiple inquiries within a short window for the same type of loan (mortgage, auto) are usually treated as one by FICO.

→ Space credit applications by 6+ months when possible.

The Truth About Credit Repair Companies

You can do everything they charge for. For free.

Legitimate credit repair companies do nothing you can't do yourself. By law, no one can legally remove accurate negative information from your credit report before its natural expiration (7 years for most items, 10 for bankruptcies). What they can do — and what you can also do — is dispute inaccurate information, negotiate pay-for-delete on old collections, and write goodwill letters. Save the $79/month.

  • Disputes: File yourself at Equifax.com, Experian.com, and TransUnion.com
  • Collections: Negotiate pay-for-delete in writing before paying
  • Goodwill letters: Ask creditors to remove a one-time late payment as a courtesy
  • Credit counseling: HUD-approved agencies offer free or low-cost help

Part Three Straight Answers
Common Questions

What people actually
want to know

Q How long does it take to raise my score 100 points?
It depends on your starting point and what's hurting you. If errors and high utilization are the main factors, 60-90 days is realistic for significant gains. If you have recent late payments or collections, 6-12 months is more typical as positive history accumulates. The lower your starting score, the faster the initial gains tend to be — there's more room to move.
Q Will checking my own credit score hurt it?
No. Checking your own credit is a "soft inquiry" and has zero effect on your score. Hard inquiries — initiated when you apply for credit — do affect your score temporarily. You can check your score as often as you want through Credit Karma, Experian, or your bank's free monitoring tool without any impact.
Q Should I pay off collections?
It depends on the age and type of collection. Under the newer FICO 9 and VantageScore 4.0 models, paid collections hurt your score less than unpaid ones. However, paying an old collection can sometimes restart the statute of limitations in your state, affecting your legal options. Before paying, try to negotiate a pay-for-delete agreement in writing — the collector agrees to remove the entry from your report entirely in exchange for payment. Get it in writing before you pay a dollar.
Q What's the difference between FICO and VantageScore?
Both use a 300-850 scale and similar factors, but they weight them differently and use different scoring models. FICO is used by about 90% of top lenders for lending decisions. VantageScore (created by the three bureaus together) is what most free monitoring services show you. Your VantageScore may differ from your FICO score by 20-50 points. Both matter, but if you're applying for a mortgage or major loan, FICO 8 or FICO 9 is what the lender sees.
Q How much does income affect my credit score?
Income is not a factor in your credit score — at all. Lenders may ask for income information separately when evaluating ability to repay, but it does not appear in your credit report and has zero weight in the score calculation. Someone earning $30,000 can have an 800 score. Someone earning $200,000 can have a 580. The score is purely about credit behavior.
Q Can closing a credit card hurt my score?
Yes, in two ways. First, it reduces your total available credit, which can increase your utilization ratio. Second, if it's an old account, it can reduce your average account age over time (closed accounts stay on your report for 10 years but eventually drop off). In general, keep cards open. If there's an annual fee you don't want to pay, ask the issuer to downgrade it to a no-fee version instead of closing it.
