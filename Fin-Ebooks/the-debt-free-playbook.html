Welcome to the playbook.

Understanding your debt is a relief. Knowing exactly what to do on Tuesday morning is a plan.

This is a working book. It assumes you have already decided you want to be done with your debt, and that what you need now is the sequence: which balance first, which calls to make, which letters to send, which traps to step around, and how to set it all up so it runs without asking you for willpower every single month.

There are 12 plays, grouped into 4 phases. Each play is one move. Each one gives you the context that matters, a tool or a script where one helps, and a place to write down what you did. Your ledger, your numbers, and your notes save automatically in this browser, on this device. Nobody else can see them, Popcycle included.

Some plays take 15 minutes. A few involve a phone call you may have been putting off. Go at whatever pace your life allows. A play a week is a perfectly good pace. So is all 12 in a weekend.

Two books, one road

If you are still carrying the weight of the debt itself (the avoidance, the knot in your stomach when the mail comes, the quiet story that you are bad with money), start with How to Get Out of Debt Without Destroying Your Life. It is the mindset half: why extreme plans fail and how to build one you can live inside.

If you are ready to put real numbers on a page and pick up the phone this week, you are in the right place. This is the tactical half.

02
The Ledger

What is your monthly attack number?

Your minimums are what you must pay. Your attack number is your minimums plus the extra you can send every month on top of them. The important word is every. Not the most you could find in a heroic month, but the amount you could repeat for 24 months without coming to resent the plan. A smaller number you keep beats a bigger number you abandon in March.

Look at where the extra will come from, line by line: a subscription cut, a grocery adjustment, a side income, a raise you already received but never redirected. Leave some breathing room in the rest of your budget on purpose. Plans with zero slack break.

Your extra each month

This is the amount above your minimums. The calculator in Play 3 uses it.

Write your attack number. Then list exactly where the extra comes from, so that future you knows which dollars are spoken for.

03
The Ledger

Which debt goes first?

There are two honest ways to order a payoff. Avalanche sends every extra dollar to the highest interest rate first, which costs you the least in total interest. Snowball sends it to the smallest balance first, which gives you your first win soonest. In both, you pay the minimum on everything else, and when a debt is gone, its whole payment rolls onto the next one. That rolling is what makes the end come faster than the beginning.

The calculator below runs both orders on your actual ledger. If the difference in interest is small, pick the one you are more likely to finish. Some people blend them: clear one tiny balance for the feeling of it, then switch to avalanche for the rest.

Your payoff, both ways

Estimates assume fixed rates, no new charges, and the same payment every month. Real life will wobble. The order is what matters.

Which order did you choose, and why? Write the first debt you will attack and the month you expect to see it reach zero.

Every point of interest you remove is money that goes to your balance instead of to the lender.

Lower the Cost.

Three plays that make each payment work harder: a phone call, an honest look at transfers and consolidation, and the automation that takes willpower out of the equation.

Play
04
Lower the Cost

Call and ask for a lower rate.

Card issuers lower interest rates for customers who ask more often than most people assume, especially for customers with a record of on-time payments. It is not guaranteed. It costs about 10 minutes and a little nerve. Start with the card at the top of your payoff order, using the number on the back of the card. Be friendly, be brief, and write down who you spoke with and what they said.

If money is tight right now, ask about hardship programs too. Many issuers offer a temporary lower rate or payment for customers going through a job loss, illness, or other disruption. Ask what the program does to the account (some freeze or close the card) before you say yes.

Say this

"Hi. I have been a customer since [year], and I have paid on time. My current rate is [APR]. I am working on paying this balance down, and I would like to ask for a lower interest rate. What are you able to do for me?"

If the answer is no

"I understand. Is there a hardship or reduced-rate program I might qualify for? And could you note my request on the account, so I can check back in a few months?"

Make one call today. Record the card, the date, the name of the person you spoke with, and the result. If it was a no, write the date you will call again.

Play
05
Lower the Cost

Does a transfer or consolidation actually save you money?

A balance transfer moves card debt to a new card with a promotional rate, often 0% for somewhere between 12 and 21 months. There is usually a transfer fee, commonly 3% to 5% of the amount moved, and whatever is left when the promotion ends starts collecting the regular rate. The test is simple: can you pay the transferred balance to zero before the promotion ends? Use the check below.

A consolidation loan rolls several debts into one fixed payment. It helps only if the new rate is genuinely lower and the term is not stretched so far that you pay more in total. Be very careful about moving card debt into a home equity loan. It can lower the rate, but it turns debt that was only on your credit into debt that is secured by your house.

One rule covers both: the card you just cleared is not new room to spend. Keep it open if it has no annual fee (closing it can lower your score), and put it somewhere out of reach.

Balance transfer check

Enter the offer terms. The answer is the monthly payment needed to clear it before the regular rate begins.

Is there a transfer or consolidation offer that passes the test for you? If so, which debt, what terms, and what monthly payment clears it in time? If not, write that down too. Deciding not to is also a decision.

Play
06
Lower the Cost

Make the whole plan automatic.

A missed payment is the most expensive mistake in a payoff plan. It can bring a late fee, a penalty rate, and, once a payment is 30 days late, a mark on your credit report. Automation takes that risk off the table and takes your mood out of the decision.

  • Autopay the minimum on every account in your ledger, so nothing is ever late.
  • Schedule the extra to go to your current target the day after payday, before the money has a chance to find another job.
  • Line up due dates. Most issuers will move your due date if you ask. Grouping them near payday makes the month calmer.
  • Plan the rollover now. Write down today which account gets the freed-up payment when your first target reaches zero, so the change takes 2 minutes when the day comes.

List every automation you set up today, with the account and the date it runs.

The accounts that frighten us most are often the ones with the most rules protecting us. Learn the rules and the fear gets smaller.

The Hard Accounts.

Collectors, medical bills, and student loans. Each runs on its own rules, and more of those rules are on your side than you may have been told.

Play
07
Hard Accounts

Before you pay a collector, validate the debt.

When a debt collector first contacts you, federal rules require them to give you a validation notice: who the original creditor was, how much they say you owe, and how to dispute it. You generally have about 30 days from receiving that notice to dispute in writing. If you do, the collector has to pause collection until they verify the debt. Debts get sold, resold, and mixed up. Asking them to show their work is your right, and it is not rude.

  • Check the age of the debt. Every state has a statute of limitations on lawsuits for old debt. In many states, a small payment or a written acknowledgment can restart that clock, so learn where you stand before you send anything.
  • Get any deal in writing first. If you negotiate a settlement or payment plan, get the agreement in writing before you pay a dollar, and keep it forever.
  • Pay in a way you control. Avoid giving a collector ongoing access to your bank account. A one-time payment you initiate keeps the control with you.
  • Never ignore a court summons. Respond by the deadline, even if you believe the debt is wrong. A missed court date can lead to a default judgment and wage garnishment.

Play
08
Hard Accounts

Is that medical bill right, and do you qualify for help?

Medical debt is the one kind of debt almost nobody chose. It also has more exits than most people realize. Start by asking the provider for an itemized bill and comparing it with your insurer's explanation of benefits. Duplicate charges and services you never received are more common than they should be.

Nonprofit hospitals, which is most hospitals, are required by federal law to have a financial assistance policy. Many people with moderate incomes qualify for a reduced bill or full forgiveness, and you can often apply even after the bill has gone to collections. Before you put a medical bill on a credit card, ask the provider for an interest-free payment plan. Most will say yes.

On your credit report: as of 2026, the 3 major credit bureaus do not report paid medical collections, medical collections under $500, or unpaid medical collections until they are at least a year old. Several states go further. If a medical debt that fits those rules shows up on your report, dispute it.

Trusted help
Dollar For (free help applying for hospital financial assistance) Check your reports for medical collections (AnnualCreditReport.com)

List each medical bill: provider, amount, whether you have the itemized bill, and whether you have asked about financial assistance. Choose one to call this week.

09
Hard Accounts

Are your student loans on the right plan for 2026?

Federal student loan repayment changed in 2026, and if you have not looked at your plan recently, it is worth an hour. The SAVE plan has ended. A new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) bases payments on 1% to 10% of your income, reduces the payment for each dependent, and waives unpaid interest. Loans taken out or consolidated on or after July 1, 2026 can use only RAP or a standard plan. Older loans may still have access to legacy plans such as IBR, while PAYE and ICR are scheduled to close in 2028.

Many readers of this book carry Parent PLUS loans taken out for a child's education. Those are not eligible for RAP, so their options are narrower and worth confirming directly. If any federal loan is in default, know that the government has resumed collecting on defaulted loans, and that rehabilitation and consolidation are both routes back to good standing. These rules have shifted several times in recent years. Confirm your options with your servicer and the official site before you change anything.

Three plays to protect your progress: a cushion so the next surprise does not land on a card, one move on the income side, and your whole playbook on a single page.

Play
10
Stay Free

What cushion keeps you off the cards?

The most common way a payoff plan fails is not a lack of discipline. It is a transmission, a dental bill, or a week of missed work that lands on a credit card and quietly erases 3 months of progress. A starter cushion of $1,000 to $2,000, built before or alongside your extra payments, is what stops that from happening.

Keep it in a separate savings account (a high-yield one if you can), not attached to your debit card, with a name that tells you what it is for. Once your high-interest debt is gone, grow it toward 3 to 6 months of your essential monthly costs. The Plain-English Guide to Building an Emergency Fund walks through that second stage.

How much is in your cushion today? What is your starter target, where will it live, and how much will you add each month until it is full?

Play
11
Stay Free

What is one income move you could make for 6 months?

There is a floor to how much you can cut, and you may already be standing on it. There is much less of a ceiling on what a few extra months of income can do. A temporary move, sent entirely to your current target, can shorten the whole timeline without touching your daily life: a stretch of overtime, a freelance project in a skill you already have, selling things that are taking up space, or a raise conversation you have been meaning to have.

Decide now what happens to windfalls (a tax refund, a bonus, a gift) before they arrive. A simple split works for most people, for example half to the debt, a quarter to the cushion, and a quarter to enjoy. Deciding in advance is what keeps the money from dissolving into the month.

Name one income move you could make for the next 6 months and roughly what it could add. Then write your windfall rule.

After Play 12 · The Long Middle

The playbook is written.

You have every balance on one page, an order you chose for your own reasons, the calls and letters that lower the cost, and a system that runs whether or not you feel motivated on a given Tuesday.

Download your playbook now so you have a copy outside this browser. Print it if you like to see it on the fridge. Come back on each check-in date, update your ledger, and watch the totals fall. The middle months are the quiet ones. They are also where most of the work gets done.

Every month you follow the plan, the next month gets a little easier.
