Powerful medications
with real trade-offs

The conversation around GLP-1s tends to swing between two extremes — "miracle drug" and "dangerous shortcut." The truth is more useful and more boring: they are genuinely effective medications that come with genuine costs, and whether they're right for you depends on weighing both honestly.

Here's the landscape as it stands. GLP-1 receptor agonists mimic a natural gut hormone that curbs appetite and regulates blood sugar. Semaglutide (sold as Ozempic for diabetes and Wegovy for weight loss) produces around 15% average weight loss in trials. Tirzepatide (Mounjaro / Zepbound), which adds a second hormone target, reaches roughly 21%. The class keeps advancing: an oral semaglutide pill and a new non-peptide oral drug arrived in 2026, and a triple-agonist in development is exceeding 28% in studies. These are not small effects — they represent a genuine shift in what's medically possible for weight and metabolic health.

But effectiveness is only half the story. These drugs carry side effects, meaningful costs and access barriers, a muscle-loss risk, and the reality that weight often returns if you stop. They aren't right for everyone, and they aren't a substitute for the habits that make any result last. This guide walks through the benefits and the drawbacks in equal measure — because an honest decision requires seeing both clearly.

The Framework

Judge them the way you'd judge any medication: benefit versus risk, for you.

Every effective medication is a trade-off — benefits weighed against side effects, costs, and risks, in the context of your particular health. GLP-1s are no different. For someone with obesity-related health conditions, the benefits may clearly outweigh the drawbacks. For someone seeking to lose a few vanity pounds, the calculus looks very different. There's no universal answer, only an individual one — which is why this guide gives you the full picture rather than a verdict.

  • Benefits scale with medical need — greatest for those with the most to gain
  • Drawbacks are real and must be planned for, not dismissed
  • The decision is medical and individual — made with a qualified provider

Part OneThe Benefits — What They Actually Do
The Upside, Honestly

Let's start with what these drugs genuinely do well — because the benefits are real, substantial, and increasingly reach beyond the number on the scale.

01Benefit
The Core Mechanism

They Quiet the "Food Noise"

The most transformative benefit many users describe isn't the weight itself — it's relief from the constant mental chatter about food. GLP-1s dial down appetite and cravings at a biological level, quieting the persistent "food noise" that many people with obesity live with every waking hour. For the first time, eating less doesn't require constant willpower, because the drive to eat is genuinely reduced. This is why the medications succeed where diets fail: they change the underlying signal rather than asking you to resist it.

Why It MattersDiets fail because they fight biology with willpower. GLP-1s change the biology — which is a fundamentally different, and more sustainable, mechanism while you're on them.
02Benefit
Substantial Weight Loss

Results That Diets Rarely Match

The weight loss is real and, for many, life-changing. Semaglutide averages around 15% of body weight in trials; tirzepatide around 21%; newer agents more still. For context, that far exceeds what lifestyle changes alone typically achieve, and it approaches what was once only possible through bariatric surgery. For someone carrying significant excess weight, losing a fifth of it can meaningfully change mobility, joint pain, energy, and health markers — benefits that compound well beyond appearance.

The ContextThese numbers are averages — some lose more, some less. But even the average represents a degree of medically meaningful weight loss that was simply not reliably achievable with medication before this class existed.
03Benefit
Beyond the Scale

Growing Health Benefits Unrelated to Weight

Increasingly, the benefits extend well past weight loss. GLP-1 medications now carry approvals or strong evidence for reducing cardiovascular events (heart attack and stroke) in at-risk patients, treating obstructive sleep apnea, improving fatty liver disease, and lowering blood sugar in type 2 diabetes — the use they were originally designed for. Some of these benefits appear to go beyond what the weight loss alone would explain. For people with these conditions, the medication may address several problems at once.

Why It MattersFor someone with diabetes, heart risk, or sleep apnea alongside excess weight, a GLP-1 may treat multiple conditions simultaneously — which substantially changes the benefit side of the equation.
04Benefit
The Psychological Shift

Relief From a Lifelong Struggle

For people who've fought their weight for decades — through countless diets, cycles of loss and regain, and the shame our culture heaps on them — GLP-1s can bring a profound psychological relief: the realization that the struggle was never a moral failing but a biological one, now treatable. Many describe finally feeling "normal" around food. That shift — from self-blame to effective treatment — is a genuine benefit that's easy to overlook when focusing only on pounds and lab values.

Why It MattersReframing obesity as a treatable medical condition rather than a character flaw is not just kind — it's accurate, and for many people it's deeply healing in its own right.

Part TwoThe Drawbacks — The Real Trade-Offs
The Downside, Honestly

The costs and risks
you need to weigh

Now the other side of the ledger — the drawbacks, in the same plain language. None of these are reasons to rule the medications out; all of them are reasons to go in informed.

1
Gastrointestinal Side Effects Are Common

The most frequent drawback is GI upset: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation, especially when starting or increasing the dose. For most people these are manageable and ease over time, particularly with slow dose escalation and eating adjustments — but for some they're significant enough to limit or stop treatment. Less commonly, more serious issues like gallstones or pancreatitis can occur. These side effects are the single most common reason people discontinue, so they're worth taking seriously and planning for.

The RealityStarting at a low dose and increasing slowly is the main tool for minimizing GI effects. Many people push through a rough early adjustment; some can't tolerate the drugs at all. You won't know your response until you try, under medical supervision.
2
Muscle Loss Is a Real Risk

This is the drawback most people aren't warned about. When you lose weight rapidly, a substantial portion of that loss — often a quarter to 40% — can come from lean muscle rather than fat. Losing muscle slows metabolism, weakens you, and undermines the very health you're trying to build, and it's a particular concern for older adults. The crucial point: this is largely preventable with adequate protein and resistance training, but it doesn't happen automatically — it requires deliberate effort alongside the medication.

The FixPrioritizing protein (roughly 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day) and doing resistance training 2–3 times a week preserves muscle while you lose fat. This isn't optional if you care about the quality of your weight loss — it's essential. (Our companion guide, Making GLP-1s Work, covers exactly how.)
3
Cost and Access Can Be Prohibitive

These medications are expensive, and insurance coverage for weight loss specifically remains inconsistent, though it's improving. Out-of-pocket costs can run into the hundreds of dollars a month. Manufacturer savings programs, compounded versions (with their own quality and legality caveats), and evolving insurance policies all affect the real price — but cost remains the single biggest barrier for many people, and it's an ongoing cost, not a one-time one. Budgeting realistically for a potentially long-term medication matters.

The RealityCheck manufacturer savings programs and compare pharmacy prices before assuming you can't afford it — but go in understanding this may be a sustained monthly expense, and factor that into the decision.
4
The Weight Often Returns If You Stop

Perhaps the most important reality to understand: for most people, the appetite suppression ends when the medication does, and studies show significant weight regain after stopping. This has reframed GLP-1s as, for many, a long-term or even indefinite treatment — like medication for blood pressure or cholesterol — rather than a short course. That's not necessarily a problem, but it changes the commitment. If the plan is to take it briefly and stop, the results are unlikely to hold without serious habit change built during treatment.

The ImplicationGo in viewing this as a potentially ongoing treatment, and use the time on the medication to build durable nutrition and exercise habits — so that whatever you decide later, you've built something that can partly stand on its own.
5
They Aren't Right for Everyone

GLP-1s have genuine contraindications and unknowns. They're not recommended for people with certain rare thyroid cancer histories (medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2 syndrome), a history of pancreatitis warrants caution, and they aren't for use in pregnancy. Because the newest agents are relatively new, the very long-term safety picture is still being filled in. And for someone without a genuine medical need, the risk-benefit balance shifts unfavorably. This is precisely why the decision belongs with a doctor who knows your full history.

The Bottom LineOnly a qualified provider can determine whether these medications are appropriate and safe given your specific health history. Certain conditions rule them out entirely; others require caution. This is not a decision to make alone or based on a website.
Straight AnswersReal Questions
Real Questions

What people actually
want to know

QHow much weight will I actually lose?
It varies by medication and individual, but trial averages give a useful guide: roughly 15% of body weight for semaglutide and around 21% for tirzepatide, with newer agents higher. Remember these are averages — some people lose considerably more, others less, and response is genuinely individual. Your results also depend heavily on what you do alongside the medication: those who add protein and resistance training tend to lose better-quality weight (more fat, less muscle) and sustain it better. Set expectations around the average, not the most dramatic before-and-after stories, which are outliers.
QHow long do I have to take it?
Potentially indefinitely, and this is one of the most important things to understand before starting. Because appetite and weight tend to return after stopping, GLP-1s are increasingly treated like ongoing medications for a chronic condition rather than a short course. Some people do taper off successfully, especially if they've built strong habits, but many stay on a maintenance dose long-term. There's nothing wrong with that — we don't expect people to stop blood-pressure medication once it works — but it's a commitment worth understanding upfront, including the ongoing cost.
QAre the compounded versions safe?
It's complicated. Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide became widely available during drug shortages and can be much cheaper, but they occupy a different regulatory space than the brand-name drugs — quality, dosing accuracy, and sourcing can vary, and they're not FDA-approved products in the same way. Some compounding is done by legitimate, regulated pharmacies; some is not. If you're considering a compounded version for cost reasons, it's essential to use a reputable, licensed source and to involve a medical provider rather than buying from questionable online sellers. Discuss the trade-offs with your doctor.
QWill insurance cover it?
Sometimes, and increasingly, but coverage for weight loss specifically remains inconsistent. Coverage is more common when the medication is prescribed for type 2 diabetes or another approved condition, and less reliable when it's purely for weight management, though this is shifting as more states and plans add obesity-medication coverage. Check your specific plan's formulary, and explore manufacturer savings programs and pharmacy price comparisons if coverage falls short. Cost is the most common practical barrier, so it's worth investigating thoroughly before assuming yes or no.
QIs it "cheating" to use a medication to lose weight?
No — and this framing deserves to be retired. We don't call it cheating to take medication for high blood pressure, diabetes, or depression, all of which have behavioral components too. Obesity is a medical condition driven substantially by biology, and treating it with an effective medical tool is medicine, not moral weakness. The "cheating" narrative is a holdover from the false belief that weight is purely a matter of willpower. Using a GLP-1 doesn't erase the value of good habits — the two work best together — but there's nothing dishonorable about treating a medical condition with medicine.
